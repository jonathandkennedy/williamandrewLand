A truck case is not a big car case

Different rules, different evidence, and a defence team already working while you are still in the emergency room.

The evidence has a shelf life

The truck records what it was doing — engine control module data, the electronic logging device, dash and cab cameras, dispatch messages. Some of it is overwritten on a timer. If nobody demands it in writing, it is gone and no one has to explain why.

They send people to the scene

Large carriers keep rapid-response teams under contract. An investigator and often a lawyer can be at the wreck the same day, building the file long before you have been discharged.

More than one company can owe you

The driver is one defendant. The motor carrier, the broker who arranged the load, the company that loaded it and the shop that serviced the brakes can each be another.

They answer to federal rules

Interstate carriers are bound by FMCSA rules: hours-of-service limits, driver qualification files, drug and alcohol testing, inspection and maintenance records. A violation in those files is not paperwork. It is the case.

The policies are much larger

Federal minimums for interstate freight start at $750,000 and run into the millions for tankers and hazardous loads. Bigger coverage means the insurer fights harder and earlier.

Utah splits the fault

Utah reduces what you recover by your share of the blame and bars recovery once your share reaches 50%. Shifting a slice of fault onto you is the cheapest move the carrier has.

What we do in the first 72 hours

A truck case is an evidence race. This part cannot wait for you to feel better.

  1. Send a spoliation letter

    A written demand that the carrier preserve the ECM download, the ELD logs, camera footage, the driver qualification file, dispatch records and the truck itself. Once served, "we lost it" stops being free.

  2. Get to the truck and the scene

    We move to inspect the tractor and trailer before they are repaired or released, and to photograph the road while the marks are still there.

  3. Identify every defendant

    We pull the police report and the carrier's USDOT filings to work out who the driver really worked for, who brokered the load, who loaded it and who maintained it.

  4. Stop the recorded statement

    The carrier's adjuster will call, friendly and fast, and ask you to describe the crash while you are on pain medication. From the day we are hired, those calls come to us.

  5. Get treatment and losses documented

    We point you to doctors who will actually see you, sort out who pays while the claim runs, and start documenting wage loss from day one.

Straight answers

The questions people actually ask on the first call.

What if the crash was partly my fault?

Call anyway. Utah reduces your recovery by your percentage of fault rather than wiping it out, and you can still recover as long as your share stays under 50%. People are also wrong about their own fault surprisingly often, usually against themselves — whether a driver had been on the road too long, or the brakes were out of adjustment, is not something you could see from your seat.

The trucking company already offered me money. Should I take it?

Not before someone reads the file. An early offer arrives before anyone knows whether your injuries settle down, and it is priced to close the claim cheaply. Accepting it usually ends the case for good. It also tends to mean they already know something about their driver or their records they would rather not hand over.

Does my immigration status affect my case?

No. Anyone injured by someone else's negligence in Utah can bring a claim, whatever their immigration status. No Social Security number or driver's licence is required. What you tell us is confidential and we do not report to immigration. This is the most common reason good cases never get made.

What does this cost me?

Nothing up front and nothing unless we win. The fee is a percentage of what we recover, agreed in writing before we start, and case costs come out of the recovery rather than your pocket. The first conversation is free and carries no obligation.

Should I talk to the trucking company's insurer?

Not before you have spoken to a lawyer. A recorded statement taken days after a crash, while you are medicated and do not yet know how badly you are hurt, is used to lock you into a version of events and to move fault onto you. You can simply say you have counsel and give them our number.

In Utah you generally have four years from the date of a crash to file an injury claim, and two years for a wrongful death claim. If a city, county or state vehicle was involved, a formal notice of claim can be due within one year — far sooner than most people expect. Deadlines turn on the facts, so confirm yours rather than assuming.
